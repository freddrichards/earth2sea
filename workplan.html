Work Plan

How Earth2Sea will connect the deep Earth, the ice sheets and the ocean to improve sea-level predictions.

The big picture

Until recently it was widely assumed that Earth’s mantle, the ~3000 km-thick layer of hot rock between the crust and the core, moves far too slowly to matter for sea-level change over decades to centuries. New observations have overturned that view. Mantle flow affects sea level and ice sheets in three important ways:

  1. It moves coastlines up and down. Slow flow in the mantle can raise or lower Earth’s surface by around 0.1 mm per year, about ten times faster than previously assumed. Ancient shorelines used to work out how high sea level was in past warm periods may therefore have shifted by several metres since they formed.
  2. It reshapes the ground beneath the ice. The stability of an ice sheet depends strongly on the shape of the bedrock it sits on and on the heat flowing up into its base. Mantle flow changes both, so ice sheets in the past may have responded to warming differently from those of today.
  3. It can push back against ice loss. When ice melts, the land underneath rebounds. Beneath parts of Antarctica and Greenland the mantle appears to be “softer” when ice is lost quickly, so the bedrock rebounds faster. This may help slow the retreat of vulnerable ice sheets, a feedback that has never been fully calculated.

Earth2Sea brings these effects together for the first time. By reconstructing how the mantle has evolved over the past 50 million years, the project will correct estimates of past sea level and ice volume, recalibrate the computer models used to forecast ice-sheet behaviour, and produce new projections of future sea-level rise that account for the dynamic Earth beneath our feet.

Deep Earth

Reconstructing 50 million years of mantle flow

We cannot sample the deep mantle directly, but earthquake waves passing through it reveal where it is hotter or colder. By combining these seismic images with laboratory experiments on how rock deforms, we will build detailed models of the mantle’s present-day temperature, density and stiffness.

Advanced computational methods will then “rewind” mantle flow back through time, producing a family of 4D reconstructions that track how Earth’s surface has been pushed up and pulled down since around 50 million years ago.

Ocean

How high were past sea levels?

Fossil shorelines and reefs from past warm periods, such as the Last Interglacial (~125,000 years ago) and the mid-Pliocene (~3 million years ago), record how high sea level once stood and so how much ice melted. But many of these markers have since been lifted or lowered by mantle flow and by the solid Earth’s response to changing ice and ocean loads.

We will compile a new global database of ancient sea-level markers, including new fieldwork in southeast Australia, and use fast machine-learning “emulators” of our Earth models to correct them. The result will be the first probabilistic estimates of global sea level during key warm periods that fully account for a moving Earth surface.

Ice

How sensitive were past ice sheets?

Computer models of ice sheets are tested by checking whether they can reproduce what happened in past warm climates. Until now, those tests have assumed that the bedrock and the heat flowing into the ice were the same in the past as today.

Using our mantle reconstructions, we will rebuild the landscape and heat flow beneath Antarctica and Greenland for past warm periods stretching back ~25 million years. Running ice-sheet models with and without these changes will reveal how much of past ice loss reflects the ice sheets’ inherent sensitivity to warming, and help pin down which models give trustworthy projections.

Future

Does a soft mantle protect ice sheets?

Measurements of rapidly rising bedrock in Antarctica suggest the mantle behaves differently depending on how quickly the load on it changes: it is effectively softer when ice is lost fast. Faster rebound lifts the bed beneath the ice, reducing contact with warm ocean water and potentially slowing runaway retreat.

We will develop new ways to include this behaviour in models of how the solid Earth deforms, couple them with ice-sheet models, and produce sea-level projections out to the year 2500 that account for the full range of mantle feedbacks. These projections will underpin a public web app showing future sea-level change along local coastlines.
