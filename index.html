Dynamic Earth Impacts on Past, Present, and Future Sea Levels

Earth2Sea links the slow churn of Earth’s mantle to the fate of the polar ice sheets, to deliver more reliable predictions of how fast, and where, sea level will rise.

About the project

Nearly 700 million people live along flood-susceptible coastlines, and actionable sea-level forecasts are urgently needed to protect these communities and their critical infrastructure. Yet current projections are highly uncertain: the last Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change report found that end-of-century sea level could lie anywhere from 0.3 to 2 m above present.

Most of this uncertainty comes from ice-sheet instabilities which, once triggered, could cause rapid sea-level rise. It can be substantially reduced by calibrating ice-sheet models so that they reproduce ice volumes during past warm periods. But these calibrations are now in question, following the discovery that flow in Earth’s mantle moves the planet’s surface up and down far faster than once thought.

This paradigm shift implies that accepted estimates of past ice volume may be too high, because the ancient shorelines used to infer them have since been uplifted by mantle flow, and that existing models miss key physics, underestimating how quickly bedrock rebounds and overestimating ice-sheet instability.

Earth2Sea will tackle these issues by combining new sea-level datasets with innovative reconstructions of mantle flow, producing probabilistic, geodynamically corrected estimates of past ice-sheet volume and sensitivity. Calibrating ice-sheet models against this revised picture of the past, and including feedbacks between the mantle and ice sheets, will yield the first geodynamically accurate global and regional sea-level projections, helping decision-makers plan long-term coastal defences.

    Project objectives

    Earth2Sea will quantify the impact of previously neglected interactions between the solid Earth and the cryosphere on sea-level predictions, through three objectives:

    1. Pin down past sea levels. Generate probabilistic estimates of peak global mean sea level during key warm periods in Earth’s history, correcting ancient shorelines for mantle-driven uplift and subsidence.
    2. Reassess past ice-sheet sensitivity. Quantify how mantle-driven changes in bedrock topography and geothermal heat flow altered the sensitivity of polar ice sheets to warming.
    3. Test whether a “soft” mantle stabilises ice sheets. Determine whether the mantle’s timescale-dependent behaviour causes faster bedrock rebound that helps protect vulnerable ice sheets, and include this in projections of future sea level.

    Results will feed an interactive web app that lets anyone see how fast sea level is likely to rise along their local coastline, and how certain we are.
